# 01. Mathematical Modelling & Vectors

## 1. Mathematical Modelling in Mechanics
Mechanics relies on simplifying physical entities using idealized mathematical concepts to construct solvable equations. Understanding the exact mechanical implications of these assumptions is vital.

###  Standard Modelling Assumptions
* **<span style="color: red;">Particle:</span>** An object whose mass is concentrated entirely at a single geometric point. 
  * *Mechanical Consequence:* Rotational effects, air resistance, and aerodynamic drag are completely neglected.
  
* **<span style="color: red;">Light Object (String, Rod, Pulley):</span>** An object modeled as having zero mass ($m = 0$).
  * *Mechanical Consequence:* The tension $T$ is uniform throughout its entire length. The force required to accelerate it is zero ($F = ma = 0 \times a = 0$).
  
* **<span style="color: red;">Inextensible/Rigid String:</span>** A string that cannot stretch under load.
  * *Mechanical Consequence:* Any two bodies connected by the string must share the exact same acceleration and velocity magnitudes along the line of the string.
  
* **<span style="color: red;">Smooth Surface:</span>** A surface that offers zero resistance to tangential motion.
  * *Mechanical Consequence:* The friction coefficient $\mu = 0$; the contact force acts purely perpendicular to the surface (Normal Reaction $R$).
  
* **<span style="color: red;">Rough Surface:</span>** A surface that exerts a resistive force parallel to the interface to oppose relative motion.
  * *Mechanical Consequence:* A frictional force $F$ is generated up to a maximum limit defined by $F_{\text{max}} = \mu R$.
  
* **<span style="color: red;">Thin/Smooth Pulley:</span>** A pulley with negligible radius and no rotational friction.
  * *Mechanical Consequence:* The tension magnitude $T$ is identical on both sides of the pulley wheel.

## 2. Vectors in 2D Mechanics
Forces, displacements, velocities, and accelerations are vector quantities possessing both magnitude and direction. In a Cartesian plane, they are resolved into orthogonal unit vectors $\mathbf{i}$ (horizontal) and $\mathbf{j}$ (vertical).

###  Structural Definitions
A generic vector $\mathbf{A}$ is written as:
$$\mathbf{A} = x\mathbf{i} + y\mathbf{j} = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}$$

* **Magnitude (Scalar Component):** Represents the absolute physical value (e.g., speed from a velocity vector).
  $$|\mathbf{A}| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$$
* **Direction Angle ($\theta$):** The angle relative to the positive horizontal axis:
  $$\theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)$$
* **Bearings:** True bearings are measured clockwise from North ($+\mathbf{j}$). Ensure you map out the sketch to resolve whether $\tan^{-1}(x/y)$ or $90^\circ - \theta$ is required.

### Kinematics Vector Application
When an object moves with a **constant velocity** vector $\mathbf{v}$, its position vector $\mathbf{r}$ at any given time $t$ relative to a fixed origin is governed by:
$$\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}_0 + \mathbf{v}t$$

Where:
* $\mathbf{r}_0$: Initial position vector $\begin{pmatrix} x_0 \\ y_0 \end{pmatrix}$ at $t = 0$.
* $\mathbf{v}$: Constant velocity vector $\begin{pmatrix} v_x \\ v_y \end{pmatrix}$.
* $\mathbf{r}$: Final position vector $\begin{pmatrix} x_t \\ y_t \end{pmatrix}$ at time $t$.

> [!TIP] Vector Interception & Collision
> For two particles $A$ and $B$ to collide, their position vectors must be identical at the same instant $t$:
> $$\mathbf{r}_A(t) = \mathbf{r}_B(t)$$
