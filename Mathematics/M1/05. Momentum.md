# Momentum

## 1. Core Foundational Definitions

### Linear Momentum
Momentum is a fundamental vector quantity that measures the quantity of motion possessed by a moving body. It is mathematically defined as the product of an object's mass and its instantaneous velocity vector:
$$\mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{v}$$

*   **Mass ($m$):** A scalar quantity measured in kilograms ($\text{kg}$).
*   **Velocity ($\mathbf{v}$):** A vector quantity measured in meters per second ($\text{m s}^{-1}$).
*   **Units:** The SI units for momentum are kilogram meters per second ($\text{kg m s}^{-1}$) or equivalently Newton-seconds ($\text{N s}$).
*   **Vector Nature:** Momentum always acts in the precise direction of the velocity vector. In a single-dimension framework, its orientation is determined purely by sign conventions ($+$ or $-$).

### Impulse
An impulse occurs when a force acts on an object over a specified time interval. It is equal to the change in momentum experienced by the object. For a constant external force $\mathbf{F}$ acting over a time duration $\Delta t$:
$$\mathbf{I} = \mathbf{F}\Delta t$$

By linking this directly with Newton's Second Law ($\mathbf{F} = \frac{\Delta \mathbf{p}}{\Delta t}$), we derive the **Impulse-Momentum Theorem**:
$$\mathbf{I} = \Delta \mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{v} - m\mathbf{u}$$

*   **$\mathbf{u}$:** Initial velocity vector before the interaction.
*   **$\mathbf{v}$:** Final velocity vector after the interaction.
*   **Vector Interpretation:** Impulse is a vector that points in the direction of the *net force*, which is identical to the direction of the *change in velocity* ($\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{u}$). It does **not** necessarily point in the direction of $\mathbf{u}$ or $\mathbf{v}$.

## 2. The Principle of Conservation of Linear Momentum (PCM)

### Theoretical Foundation
Derived directly from Newton's Third Law (every action has an equal and opposite reaction), the total linear momentum of an isolated system of interacting particles remains constant, provided no external forces act on the system.

During a collision between Particle A and Particle B:
$$\mathbf{F}_{B\text{ on } A} = -\mathbf{F}_{A\text{ on } B}$$
$$\mathbf{F}_{B\text{ on } A}\Delta t = -\mathbf{F}_{A\text{ on } B}\Delta t$$
$$\mathbf{I}_A = -\mathbf{I}_B$$

Thus, the momentum lost by one particle is exactly gained by the other particle.

### General Scalar Equation (1D Systems)
For two bodies moving along a single straight line:
$$m_1 u_1 + m_2 u_2 = m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2$$

> [!WARNING] Sign Convention Trap
> You must establish a fixed positive direction (e.g., Left-to-Right is positive) *before* performing any calculations. If a particle changes direction or travels backwards, its velocity must be entered as a negative scalar value. Failure to assign signs properly is the most common cause of errors in collision problems.

## 3. Classification of 1D Collision Scenarios

### Scenario A: Distinct Post-Collision Velocities
The particles collide and bounce off one another, remaining separate entities with distinct final velocities ($v_1 \neq v_2$).
*   **Governing Equation:** $m_1 u_1 + m_2 u_2 = m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2$
*   *Note:* If $v_1$ or $v_2$ are unknown, additional details (such as the magnitude of the impulse or kinetic energy transformations) must be supplied to solve the system.

### Scenario B: Coalescence (Joining Together)
The particles collide and couple together, moving as a single combined mass with a shared final velocity ($v$).
*   **Governing Equation:** 
    $$m_1 u_1 + m_2 u_2 = (m_1 + m_2)v$$
*   **Resulting Shared Velocity:**
    $$v = \frac{m_1 u_1 + m_2 u_2}{m_1 + m_2}$$

### Scenario C: Explosions or Separations
A single composite system initially at rest (or traveling at a constant velocity) suddenly splits apart into two or more distinct components due to an internal release of energy (e.g., a string burning between two compressed trolleys).
*   **Governing Equation (Starting from rest):**
    $$0 = m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2 \implies m_1 v_1 = -m_2 v_2$$
*   The components move away from each other in opposite directions, with their speeds inversely proportional to their respective masses.

## 4. Two-Dimensional (2D) Vector Collisions

When motion occurs across a plane, velocities are expressed using $\mathbf{i}$-$\mathbf{j}$ unit vectors or column vector notation. The Principle of Conservation of Momentum holds independently along each orthogonal axis.

### Vector PCM Formula
$$m_1 \mathbf{u}_1 + m_2 \mathbf{u}_2 = m_1 \mathbf{v}_1 + m_2 \mathbf{v}_2$$

Written in explicitly expanded component form:
$$m_1 \begin{pmatrix} u_{1x} \\ u_{1y} \end{pmatrix} + m_2 \begin{pmatrix} u_{2x} \\ u_{2y} \end{pmatrix} = m_1 \begin{pmatrix} v_{1x} \\ v_{1y} \end{pmatrix} + m_2 \begin{pmatrix} v_{2x} \\ v_{2y} \end{pmatrix}$$

### Vector Impulse Calculations
When computing a 2D impulse, track the horizontal and vertical velocity components separately:
$$\mathbf{I} = m(\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{u}) = m \left[ \begin{pmatrix} v_x \\ v_y \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} u_x \\ u_y \end{pmatrix} \right] = \begin{pmatrix} I_x \\ I_y \end{pmatrix}$$

*   **Magnitude of Impulse:** $\vert\mathbf{I}\vert = \sqrt{I_x^2 + I_y^2}$
*   **Direction of Impulse:** Calculated using trigonometry relative to the coordinate axes: $\theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\vert I_y \vert}{\vert I_x \vert}\right)$

## 5. Comprehensive Worked Exemplar

### Problem Statement
A particle $A$ of mass $0.4\text{ kg}$ is moving along a smooth horizontal straight path with a velocity of $6\text{ m s}^{-1}$. It collides directly with a particle $B$ of mass $0.6\text{ kg}$ which is moving along the same path in the opposite direction with a speed of $2\text{ m s}^{-1}$. 

In the collision, the direction of motion of $A$ is reversed, and its speed immediately after the collision is $1.5\text{ m s}^{-1}$.

Find:
1.  The velocity of $B$ immediately after the collision.
2.  The magnitude of the impulse exerted on $A$ during the collision.

### Systematic Solution

#### Step 1: Establish Sign Conventions & Extract Variables
Let the initial direction of motion of particle $A$ be the **positive direction** ($+$).

*   **Particle A:**
    *   Mass $m_A = 0.4\text{ kg}$
    *   Initial Velocity $u_A = +6\text{ m s}^{-1}$
    *   Final Velocity $v_A = -1.5\text{ m s}^{-1}$ *(Reversed direction)*
*   **Particle B:**
    *   Mass $m_B = 0.6\text{ kg}$
    *   Initial Velocity $u_B = -2\text{ m s}^{-1}$ *(Opposite direction)*
    *   Final Velocity $v_B = ?$

#### Step 2: Apply the Principle of Conservation of Momentum
$$m_A u_A + m_B u_B = m_A v_A + m_B v_B$$

Substitute the scalar values carefully with their signs:
$$(0.4)(6) + (0.6)(-2) = (0.4)(-1.5) + (0.6)(v_B)$$
$$2.4 - 1.2 = -0.6 + 0.6v_B$$
$$1.2 = -0.6 + 0.6v_B$$

Isolate $v_B$:
$$1.2 + 0.6 = 0.6v_B$$
$$1.8 = 0.6v_B$$
$$v_B = \frac{1.8}{0.6} = +3\text{ m s}^{-1}$$

> [!TIP] Vector Interpretation of Result
> Since $v_B$ is positive ($+3\text{ m s}^{-1}$), particle $B$ has reversed its original direction of motion and is now moving in the direction that $A$ was initially traveling.

#### Step 3: Calculate the Impulse Exerted on Particle A
$$\mathbf{I}_A = m_A(v_A - u_A)$$
$$I_A = 0.4(-1.5 - 6)$$
$$I_A = 0.4(-7.5) = -3\text{ N s}$$

Since the question asks for the **magnitude** of the impulse:
$$\vert I_A \vert = 3\text{ N s}$$

*(Note: The negative sign indicates that the impulse acts in the negative direction, pushing against $A$'s original forward motion to reverse it).*
